## 11.1 Introduction to Ethics

- **Ethics** consists of the standards of behaviour we hold ourselves to in our personal and professional lives, setting our levels of honesty, empathy, and trustworthiness.
- In business, ethics guides how we treat customers, clients, colleagues, employees, and shareholders. The chapter asks you to define what career success means to you and how ethics fits in.

## 11.2 Being a Professional of Integrity

- **Stakeholders** are everyone affected by a business’s decisions: clients, customers, suppliers, investors, retailers, employees, the media, government, the community, competitors, and the environment. Patagonia gives 1% of sales to the planet and is a market leader.
- **Business ethics** guides how companies obey the law and respect stakeholders’ rights. The text uses ethics and morals interchangeably and says religious belief isn’t required to act ethically.
- **Integrity** is unity between what we say and what we do: adhering to a code of ethics, being trustworthy and incorruptible, practising what you preach. Convicted fraudster Mark Faris named greed, arrogance, and ambition, and the human ability to rationalize, as causes.
- **Legal compliance** is mandatory and is only the baseline minimum. **Ethical behaviour** builds above it and is voluntary and personal. Something can be legal yet unethical, and ethical thinking often comes before changes in the law. Medicine has the Hippocratic Oath; business has company codes such as Johnson & Johnson’s Credo.
- **Normative** ethical theories say how people *ought* to behave; **descriptive** theories (from psychology) describe how they *do* behave. Three normative approaches: **utilitarianism** (judge the ends or consequences: the greatest good for the greatest number), **deontology** (judge the means: duties and universal rules; Immanuel Kant; “deon” means duty), and **virtue ethics** (judge the character of the decision-maker, built through habits; cardinal virtues are courage, self-control, justice, and wisdom).
- **Phronesis** is Aristotle’s practical wisdom: knowledge from experience that lets leaders make prudent, values-guided judgments. Wise leaders practise moral discernment, assess situations quickly, create shared purpose, communicate with stories and metaphors, and develop wisdom in others. The first question for a company: “Do we have a moral purpose?”
- AI ethics: firms should set guidelines, design inclusively, audit for bias, govern data, and keep educating staff. Cautionary examples: Amazon’s recruiting tool that was biased against women, Cambridge Analytica’s misuse of data, the GDPR’s “right to explanation,” Microsoft’s Tay chatbot, and Google’s Project Maven.

## 11.3 Ethics and Profitability

- Focusing only on quarterly profits creates a short-term perspective. Measuring true profitability requires a **long-term perspective**: going green, social responsibility, and satisfying customers cost money now but pay off later.
- Milton Friedman wrote in 1970 that the only social responsibility of business is to increase its profits. Most commentators now say ethical leadership means doing right by all stakeholders, and that firms do better over the long run when they do.
- The **social contract** is an implicit agreement among members of society to cooperate for mutual benefit. In return for society letting it exist, a company owes something back. Whistleblower Bradley Birkenfeld exposed UBS’s illegal offshore tax business: UBS was fined \$780 million and he received a \$104 million reward, after serving 40 months in prison himself.
- Toyota sold cars in the U.S. at a slight loss for years after 1957 to build trust, part of “The Toyota Way.” The most admired companies tend to have the highest profit margins.
- Warren Buffett’s tests: do nothing you wouldn’t want an unfriendly but intelligent reporter to put on the front page; “Lose money for the firm … and I will be understanding; lose reputation … and I will be ruthless.”
- **Stockholders** are just one group of stakeholders; considering only them in the short term is a common management error. A three-column analysis helps: list stakeholders by priority, their interests, and the likely impact of the decision.
- **Goodwill** is the positive feeling stakeholders have toward a company: an intangible asset including reputation, brand value, workforce quality, and customer loyalty. It takes a long time to build. Equifax’s 2017 breach exposed data on nearly half of Americans; executives sold about \$2 million in stock before it was announced, shares fell about 14%, and the CEO resigned.
- **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** means a business sees itself as a member of society with social and environmental obligations beyond the law, so maximizing shareholder wealth can’t be its only purpose. Doing good while doing well: TOMS gives a pair of shoes for every pair sold (75 million+ pairs by 2018).

## 11.4 Ethical Dilemmas

- Rushworth Kidder distinguishes an **ethical dilemma** (right versus right: two ethical principles conflict) from a **moral temptation** (right versus wrong: breaking a law, departing from the truth, or straying from integrity).
- Kidder’s nine steps: (1) identify the moral issue, (2) determine responsibility, (3) collect information, (4) distinguish right-versus-wrong issues, (5) evaluate right-versus-right paradigms, (6) apply resolution principles, (7) explore alternative solutions, (8) choose a course of action, (9) reflect on the action.
- Step 4 tests point to a moral temptation if: a law is broken (**legal test**), a regulation or code of conduct is broken (**regulations test**), you have a gut feeling against it (**stench test**), you wouldn’t want it on the front page (**front-page test**), or you wouldn’t tell a moral exemplar (**mom test**). If it is a moral temptation, the right choice is clear and you stop there.
- Right-versus-right paradigms: **truth vs. loyalty**, **self vs. community**, **short-term vs. long-term**, and **justice vs. mercy**.
- Resolution principles come from the three normative theories: utilitarianism (examine consequences), deontology (consider the duties you owe others), and virtue ethics (your actions determine who you are). Then look for a creative third option.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ethics** | The standards of behaviour we hold ourselves to in our personal and professional lives. |
| **Stakeholders** | All individuals and entities affected by a business’s decisions, from customers and employees to the community and environment. |
| **Business ethics** | The conduct by which companies obey the law and respect the rights of their stakeholders. |
| **Integrity** | Unity between what we say and what we do; adhering to a code of ethics. |
| **Legal compliance** | Conducting business according to applicable laws and regulations; mandatory, and only the baseline minimum. |
| **Normative ethical theories** | Theories about how people ought to behave. |
| **Descriptive ethical theories** | Theories, based on scientific evidence, about how people tend to behave. |
| **Utilitarianism** | Judging an action by its consequences; the ethical choice produces the greatest good for the greatest number. |
| **Deontology** | Judging an action by its means; we owe duties to others and must follow universal rules. Associated with Kant. |
| **Virtue ethics** | Judging decisions by the character of the decision-maker, built through habits. |
| **Cardinal virtues** | Courage, self-control, justice, and wisdom. |
| **Phronesis** | Aristotle’s practical wisdom: experience-based knowledge for making prudent, values-guided judgments. |
| **Social contract** | An implicit agreement among members of society to cooperate for mutual benefit; a business owes society something in return. |
| **Long-term perspective** | Measuring success over a longer period, recognizing that the effects of decisions may take time to appear. |
| **Stockholders** | Individuals and institutions that own shares of a corporation; one group of stakeholders. |
| **Goodwill** | The positive feeling stakeholders have for a company, including its reputation, brand, and customer loyalty. |
| **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** | A business seeing itself as a member of society with social and environmental obligations beyond the law. |
| **Whistleblower** | An insider who reports an organization’s wrongdoing to authorities. |
| **Ethical dilemma** | A right-versus-right conflict in which two ethical principles clash. |
| **Moral temptation** | A right-versus-wrong choice involving breaking a law, departing from the truth, or straying from integrity. |
| **Stench test** | Asking whether you have a gut feeling that one option is wrong. |
| **Front-page test** | Asking whether you’d be comfortable seeing the issue on the front page of a newspaper. |
| **Mom test** | Asking whether you’d be comfortable telling a moral exemplar about the issue. |
| **Truth versus loyalty** | A right-versus-right paradigm: being honest versus staying loyal to someone. |
| **Justice versus mercy** | A right-versus-right paradigm: applying the rules versus showing compassion. |
| **Short-term versus long-term** | A right-versus-right paradigm: meeting an immediate need versus building a lasting solution. |

### Multiple choice

**1. Which best defines ethics?**

A. The laws that govern business  
B. A company’s marketing plan  
C. The standards of behaviour we hold ourselves to in personal and professional life  
D. The study of profit maximization  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. The standards of behaviour we hold ourselves to in personal and professional life.** Ethics sets our levels of honesty, empathy, and trustworthiness.

**2. Which statement about stakeholders is correct?**

A. Stakeholders are people who own company stock  
B. Employees are not stakeholders  
C. Only shareholders are stakeholders  
D. Competitors and the environment can be stakeholders  

??? success "Show answer"
    **D. Competitors and the environment can be stakeholders.** Stakeholders are everyone affected by a business’s decisions.

**3. Integrity is best described as:**

A. Following only the law  
B. Unity between what we say and what we do  
C. Earning the highest profits  
D. Loyalty to shareholders above all  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Unity between what we say and what we do.** It implies trustworthiness and incorruptibility.

**4. How does the textbook describe legal compliance compared with ethics?**

A. They mean exactly the same thing  
B. Legal compliance is always higher than ethical standards  
C. Ethics is mandatory and law is voluntary  
D. Legal compliance is a mandatory baseline; ethics is voluntary and goes beyond it  

??? success "Show answer"
    **D. Legal compliance is a mandatory baseline; ethics is voluntary and goes beyond it.** An action can be legal but unethical.

**5. Which approach judges an action by whether it produces the greatest good for the greatest number?**

A. Descriptive ethics  
B. Utilitarianism  
C. Deontology  
D. Virtue ethics  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Utilitarianism.** It focuses on ends, or consequences.

**6. Which approach says we owe duties to others and must follow universal rules, regardless of outcomes?**

A. Deontology  
B. Consequentialism  
C. Virtue ethics  
D. Utilitarianism  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Deontology.** Associated with Immanuel Kant; “deon” is Greek for duty.

**7. Which approach focuses on the character of the decision-maker, built through habits?**

A. Deontology  
B. Virtue ethics  
C. Legal compliance  
D. Utilitarianism  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Virtue ethics.** A child taught to return borrowed toys may grow into a trustworthy adult.

**8. The cardinal virtues are:**

A. Truth, loyalty, justice, and mercy  
B. Courage, self-control, justice, and wisdom  
C. Profit, growth, market share, and loyalty  
D. Honesty, speed, cost, and quality  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Courage, self-control, justice, and wisdom.** They come from the Greek philosophical tradition.

**9. Theories that describe how people tend to behave, based on psychology, are:**

A. Cardinal virtues  
B. Normative ethical theories  
C. Descriptive ethical theories  
D. Resolution principles  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. Descriptive ethical theories.** Normative theories say how people ought to behave.

**10. Aristotle’s “practical wisdom” that helps leaders make prudent, values-based judgments is:**

A. Goodwill  
B. Deontology  
C. Phronesis  
D. The social contract  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. Phronesis.** Nonaka and Takeuchi call it the forgotten kind of knowledge CEOs need.

**11. Amazon scrapped an AI recruiting tool because:**

A. It learned bias against women from historical hiring data  
B. It violated the GDPR’s right to explanation  
C. It hired too many people  
D. It cost too much to run  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. It learned bias against women from historical hiring data.** AI is only as unbiased as its training data.

**12. Milton Friedman argued in 1970 that the only social responsibility of business is to:**

A. Donate to charity  
B. Increase its profits  
C. Protect the environment  
D. Serve all stakeholders equally  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Increase its profits.** Most commentators today take a broader stakeholder view.

**13. The implicit agreement that a business owes something back to the society that lets it exist is the:**

A. Code of conduct  
B. Social contract  
C. Partnership agreement  
D. Labour agreement  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Social contract.** A company gives back while it enriches itself.

**14. Why did Toyota sell cars in the U.S. at a slight loss for years after 1957?**

**15. Warren Buffett told managers to do nothing they wouldn’t want:**

B. An unfriendly but intelligent reporter to write about on the front page

**16. The positive feeling stakeholders have toward a company, including reputation and customer loyalty, is:**

A. Goodwill  
B. Gross margin

**17. What did Equifax executives do that raised ethical concerns after the 2017 data breach?**

**18. A business that sees itself as a member of society with social and environmental obligations beyond the law practises:**

A. Utilitarian pricing

**19. TOMS giving a pair of shoes to a child in need for every pair sold is an example of:**

A. Corporate social responsibility  
B. A moral temptation  
C. Market penetration only  
D. Predatory dumping

    **A. Corporate social responsibility.** It combines for-profit and nonprofit thinking.

**20. According to Kidder, an ethical dilemma is:**

A. A right-versus-right conflict between two ethical principles  
B. A disagreement about price  
C. Any illegal act  
D. A right-versus-wrong choice  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. A right-versus-right conflict between two ethical principles.** A moral temptation is right versus wrong.

**21. You realize the option you’re considering breaks a company regulation. Kidder would call this:**

A. A moral temptation  
B. A justice-versus-mercy paradigm  
C. An ethical dilemma  
D. A utilitarian solution  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. A moral temptation.** Failing the regulations test signals right versus wrong.

**22. Asking “Would I want to tell a moral exemplar about this?” is the:**

A. Mom test  
B. Front-page test  
C. Stench test  
D. Legal test  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Mom test.** If you wouldn’t, it’s likely a moral temptation.

**23. Being honest with a stranger versus staying loyal to a long-time friend is which paradigm?**

A. Truth versus loyalty  
B. Justice versus mercy  
C. Short-term versus long-term  
D. Self versus community  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Truth versus loyalty.** Both choices have ethical merit.

**24. Supplying bottled water now versus building a purification system is which paradigm?**

A. Self versus community  
B. Truth versus loyalty  
C. Justice versus mercy  
D. Short-term versus long-term  

??? success "Show answer"
    **D. Short-term versus long-term.** It weighs an immediate need against a lasting solution.

**25. If your analysis shows the issue is a moral temptation, what should you do?**

A. Stop; the right option is clear, so choose it  
B. Continue through all nine steps

    **A. Stop; the right option is clear, so choose it.** Only ethical dilemmas need the remaining steps.
