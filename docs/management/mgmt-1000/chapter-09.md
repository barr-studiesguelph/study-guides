# Chapter 9: Understanding Accounting

!!! abstract "Chapter overview"
    What accounting is and who uses it, the three main financial statements and the accounting equation, and how to analyze statements with horizontal, vertical, and ratio analysis. This chapter is the basis for the case study.

## 9.1 Defining Accounting

- Accounting is called “the language of business.” It is the process of **measuring and summarizing business activities, interpreting financial information, and communicating the results** to management and other decision-makers.
- **Stakeholders** such as owners, managers, and investors are affected by a business’s activities and rely on accounting information to make better decisions.
- Collecting the numbers is the easy part. The hard part is analyzing, interpreting, and communicating what they mean.

## 9.2 Fields of Accounting

- **Management (managerial) accountants** give information and analysis to decision-makers *inside* the organization. Reports are flexible and tailored to each manager’s needs, and they work with every functional area.
- **Financial accountants** serve people inside and outside the firm, with a primary focus on *external* parties. They prepare the financial statements: the income statement (profit/loss statement), statement of owner’s equity, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows. Public companies (for example, on the TSX) must make these public; internal management reports are not.
- Canadian financial accountants follow **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)**, so statements are accurate and comparable across companies. Public companies often publish detailed IFRS statements plus simplified versions for the general public in annual reports.
- Users of financial statements: owners and managers (a report card), investors and creditors (Warren Buffett says to learn all the accounting you can; he turned \$10,000 into \$66 billion), government agencies (provincial securities commissions such as the Ontario Securities Commission, and the Canada Revenue Agency), suppliers (can the company pay its bills?), and employees and unions (pay depends on performance).

## 9.3 The Accounting Equation & Financial Statements

- Executives can no longer plead ignorance about their firm’s financial reports. Three statements matter most here: the **income statement** (sales, expenses, and whether a profit was made), the **balance sheet** (assets, liabilities, and owner’s investment), and the **statement of cash flows** (cash coming in and going out).
- In accounting, **cash** means more than bills and coins: it includes chequing and savings balances and items you can deposit, such as cheques and money orders.
- Income statement logic: **Sales − Cost of goods sold = Gross profit** (gross margin). **Gross profit − Operating expenses = Net income**, the bottom line (or a net loss). Cost of goods sold is the cost of the items sold; operating expenses are all other costs of running the business.
- Stress-Buster example: each pack costs \$6 (a \$1 chest plus five \$1 toys) and sells for \$10. Monthly operating expenses are \$300 (table \$20, wages \$240 = 32 hours × \$7.50, ads \$40). Selling 100 packs: sales \$1,000 − COGS \$600 = gross profit \$400 − \$300 = **net income \$100**.
- The balance sheet shows **assets** (resources expected to provide future benefit), **liabilities** (debts owed to outsiders), and **owner’s equity** (the owner’s investment) at a *point in time*. The income statement covers a *period* of time.
- The **accounting equation**: **Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity**. Both sides must balance. Stress-Buster on April 1: cash \$600 = \$400 loan + \$200 owner’s money. On April 30, after keeping the \$100 profit: cash \$700 = \$400 + \$300. Retained profit raises both assets and equity.
- Statements are prepared at least yearly; a firm usually ends its **fiscal year** after its peak selling period (a crab processor in October). Many also report monthly or quarterly.
- The statement of cash flows groups cash into **operating**, **investing** (buying equipment or other long-term assets), and **financing** (loans and owner investment) activities. Stress-Buster: operating \$100 + investing \$0 + financing \$600 = ending cash \$700.

## 9.4 Analyzing Financial Statements

- **Horizontal analysis** compares figures across years. Apple’s sales grew 12.7% from 2016 to 2017. Growth % = (This year − Last year) ÷ Last year × 100.
- **Vertical analysis** expresses a figure as a percent of a larger figure in the same year, such as net income as a % of sales or cash as a % of total assets. **Return on sales** = Net income ÷ Sales; Apple’s was 22.7% in 2017, more than \$1 of profit per \$5 of sales.
- **Ratio analysis** divides one number by another. One ratio alone says little; the value lies in trends over time and comparisons with other companies.
- Four categories: **profitability ratios** (profit relative to investment or sales), **liquidity ratios** (ability to pay bills soon; liquidity is how quickly an asset becomes cash, and stock is more liquid than a building), **debt ratios** (how much borrowing finances the business; more debt means more risk), and **efficiency ratios** (how well assets are managed; beyond this course).
- **Earnings per share (EPS)** = Net income ÷ Shares outstanding. Apple’s rose from \$3.38 to \$3.92, about 15%.
- **Current ratio** = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities. Apple: \$68.5B ÷ \$63.5B = 1.08. Above 1 means it can cover its current bills. Apple also held \$207.9 billion of long-term marketable securities as extra cushion.
- **Debt-to-equity ratio** = Total liabilities ÷ Owner’s equity. Use *total* liabilities, not just long-term debt. Apple: \$266.6B ÷ \$140.2B = 1.9, so it had borrowed more than owners invested, though its securities could pay debt down.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Accounting** | Measuring and summarizing business activities, interpreting financial information, and communicating the results to decision-makers. |
| **Management accounting** | Accounting that provides flexible, tailored information to decision-makers inside the organization. |
| **Financial accounting** | Accounting that prepares financial statements for users inside and especially outside the organization. |
| **IFRS** | International Financial Reporting Standards, the uniform rules Canadian financial accountants follow so statements are accurate and comparable. |
| **Income statement** | A statement of sales, expenses, and profit or loss over a period of time; also called the profit/loss statement. |
| **Balance sheet** | A statement of assets, liabilities, and owner’s equity at a specific point in time. |
| **Statement of cash flows** | A statement of cash coming in and going out from operating, investing, and financing activities. |
| **Cost of goods sold** | The total cost of the goods a business has sold. |
| **Gross profit** | Sales minus cost of goods sold; also called gross margin. |
| **Operating expenses** | The costs of operating the business other than the cost of the goods sold. |
| **Net income** | Gross profit minus operating expenses (and other expenses); the bottom line. |
| **Assets** | Resources from which a business expects to gain some future benefit. |
| **Liabilities** | Debts a business owes to outside individuals or organizations. |
| **Owner’s equity** | The owners’ investment in the business, including profits kept in it. |
| **Accounting equation** | Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity. |
| **Fiscal year** | The 12-month accounting period a company reports on, often ending after its peak selling period. |
| **Operating activities** | Cash flows from the business’s day-to-day operations, such as net income. |
| **Investing activities** | Cash flows from buying or selling long-term assets such as equipment and machinery. |
| **Financing activities** | Cash flows from borrowing, repaying loans, and owner investment. |
| **Horizontal analysis** | Comparing financial figures from one year to the next. |
| **Vertical analysis** | Expressing a figure as a percent of a larger figure in the same period, such as net income as a percent of sales. |
| **Ratio analysis** | Evaluating a company’s financial performance by dividing one financial figure by another. |
| **Return on sales** | Net income divided by sales, shown as a percent. |
| **Earnings per share (EPS)** | Net income divided by the number of shares outstanding. |
| **Liquidity** | How quickly an asset can be turned into cash. |
| **Current ratio** | Current assets divided by current liabilities; a measure of ability to pay near-term bills. |
| **Debt-to-equity ratio** | Total liabilities divided by owner’s equity; shows how a company is financed. |
| **Profitability ratios** | Ratios showing profit relative to the amount invested or sold. |
| **Liquidity ratios** | Ratios showing how well positioned a company is to pay its bills in the near future. |
| **Debt ratios** | Ratios showing how much borrowing a company uses to finance its operations. |
| **Efficiency ratios** | Ratios showing how well a company’s assets are being managed. |

### Multiple choice

**1. Accounting is often called:**

A. The study of scarcity  
B. The science of profit  
C. The language of business  
D. The art of marketing  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. The language of business.** It communicates what owners, managers, and investors need to evaluate performance.

**2. Accountants who prepare flexible, tailored reports for managers inside the organization are:**

A. Auditors for the CRA  
B. Management accountants  
C. Securities commissioners  
D. Financial accountants  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Management accountants.** Financial accountants focus mainly on external users.

**3. The primary focus of financial accountants is:**

A. Only the firm’s own managers  
B. External parties such as investors and creditors  
C. Marketing research  
D. Setting employee pay  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. External parties such as investors and creditors.** Management accounting keeps the business running; financial accounting tells the outside world how well it’s run.

**4. Canadian financial accountants prepare statements according to:**

A. International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)  
B. The Ansoff Matrix  
C. The Uruguay Round  
D. The Canada Revenue Agency’s budget  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS).** Common rules make statements accurate and comparable.

**5. Which statement shows assets, liabilities, and owner’s equity at a specific point in time?**

A. Income statement  
B. Balance sheet  
C. Statement of cash flows  
D. Master production schedule  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Balance sheet.** The income statement covers a period of time.

**6. Which statement shows sales, expenses, and whether a profit was made?**

A. Income statement  
B. Statement of cash flows  
C. Balance sheet  
D. Bill of materials  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Income statement.** It is also called the profit/loss statement.

**7. A business has sales of \$1,000, cost of goods sold of \$600, and operating expenses of \$300. What is its gross profit?**

A. \$100  
B. \$400  
C. \$700  
D. \$1,000  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. \$400.** Gross profit = Sales − COGS = \$1,000 − \$600.

**8. Using the same figures (sales \$1,000, COGS \$600, operating expenses \$300), what is net income?**

A. \$400  
B. \$700  
C. \$100  
D. \$300  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. \$100.** Net income = Gross profit − Operating expenses = \$400 − \$300.

**9. A company has assets of \$700 and liabilities of \$400. What is owner’s equity?**

A. \$1,100  
B. \$300  
C. \$400  
D. \$700  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. \$300.** Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s equity, so equity = \$700 − \$400.

**10. Which is the accounting equation?**

A. Assets = Revenue − Expenses  
B. Liabilities = Assets + Owner’s Equity  
C. Owner’s Equity = Sales − Cost of goods sold  
D. Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity  

??? success "Show answer"
    **D. Assets = Liabilities + Owner’s Equity.** Assets came either from owners or from lenders.

**11. The \$400 Stress-Buster borrowed to start the business is reported in which cash flow section?**

A. Cost of goods sold  
B. Financing activities  
C. Investing activities  
D. Operating activities  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. Financing activities.** Loans and owner investment are financing.

**12. Cash spent buying new equipment appears under:**

A. Investing activities  
B. Operating activities  
C. Gross profit  
D. Financing activities  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Investing activities.** Stress-Buster bought no equipment, so it reported \$0 investing.

**13. Comparing this year’s sales with last year’s sales is:**

A. Benchmarking  
B. Vertical analysis  
C. Horizontal analysis  
D. Ratio of liquidity  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. Horizontal analysis.** Apple’s 12.7% sales growth is a horizontal analysis.

**14. Expressing cash as a percentage of total assets is:**

A. Horizontal analysis  
B. Break-even analysis  
C. Vertical analysis  
D. Trend analysis  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. Vertical analysis.** Vertical analysis compares a figure with a larger figure in the same period.

**15. The current ratio is calculated as:**

A. Current assets ÷ Current liabilities  
B. Total liabilities ÷ Owner’s equity  
C. Net income ÷ Shares outstanding  
D. Net income ÷ Sales  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Current assets ÷ Current liabilities.** It measures ability to pay current bills.

**16. A firm has current assets of \$90,000 and current liabilities of \$60,000. Its current ratio is:**

A. 0.67  
B. 1.5  
C. 150  
D. 30,000  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. 1.5.** \$90,000 ÷ \$60,000 = 1.5.

**17. A current ratio greater than 1 means the company:**

A. Has more current assets than current liabilities, so it can pay its near-term bills  
B. Has no long-term debt  
C. Is losing money  
D. Has borrowed more than owners invested  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Has more current assets than current liabilities, so it can pay its near-term bills.** Suppliers selling on credit would feel secure.

**18. When calculating debt-to-equity, the textbook says to use:**

A. Total liabilities, not just long-term debt  
B. Total assets  
C. Only long-term debt  
D. Only current liabilities  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. Total liabilities, not just long-term debt.** Amounts owed but not yet paid are also borrowing.

**19. Total liabilities are \$300,000 and owner’s equity is \$200,000. The debt-to-equity ratio and its meaning:**

A. 500,000, so the company is very profitable  
B. 1.5, so the company has borrowed more than owners have invested  
C. 1.5, so the company can pay its current bills  
D. 0.67, so owners have invested more than was borrowed  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. 1.5, so the company has borrowed more than owners have invested.** \$300,000 ÷ \$200,000 = 1.5, which is above 1.

**20. Net income is \$2,000,000 and there are 500,000 shares outstanding. EPS is:**

A. \$4.00  
B. \$2.50  
C. \$40.00  
D. \$0.25  

??? success "Show answer"
    **A. \$4.00.** EPS = Net income ÷ Shares outstanding.

**21. Net income is \$50,000 on sales of \$400,000. Return on sales is:**

A. 1.25%  
B. 8%  
C. 80%  
D. 12.5%  

??? success "Show answer"
    **D. 12.5%.** \$50,000 ÷ \$400,000 = 0.125 = 12.5%.

**22. Which asset is the most liquid?**

A. A building  
B. Land  
C. A machine  
D. Shares of stock  

??? success "Show answer"
    **D. Shares of stock.** Liquidity is how quickly an asset can be turned into cash.

**23. Ratios that show how well a company can pay its bills in the near future are:**

A. Debt ratios  
B. Profitability ratios  
C. Liquidity ratios  
D. Efficiency ratios  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. Liquidity ratios.** The current ratio is the key example.

**24. Which agency receives annual financial reports from public companies based in Ontario?**

A. Statistics Canada  
B. The Bank of Canada  
C. The Ontario Securities Commission  
D. The World Trade Organization  

??? success "Show answer"
    **C. The Ontario Securities Commission.** Companies must also file financial information with the Canada Revenue Agency.

**25. Why might a seasonal business end its fiscal year in a particular month?**

A. Because IFRS requires December 31  
B. To match the end of its peak selling period  
C. To raise its current ratio  
D. To avoid filing taxes  

??? success "Show answer"
    **B. To match the end of its peak selling period.** A crab processor might end its year in October.

**26. According to the textbook, the deeper value of ratio analysis comes from:**

A. Using only the income statement  
B. Looking at trends over time and comparing with other companies

    **B. Looking at trends over time and comparing with other companies.** One ratio for one period tells you little.
